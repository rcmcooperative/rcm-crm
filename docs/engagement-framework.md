# Engagement Framework

This document describes the conceptual frameworks the RCM CRM uses to understand and prioritise community engagement.
It is referenced by both `why-crm.md` and `architecture-technical.md`, and is intended to stand alone as reference material beyond the CRM itself.

## Mountain of Engagement

Originally developed by Chad Sansing and Abigail Cabunoc Mayes (Mozilla Open Leadership and Open Seeds), adapted by Cass Gould van Praag for RCM Cooperative use.
It describes a progression toward community leadership:

1. **Discovery** — the person becomes known to us
2. **First contact** — first engagement, for example a mailing list sign-up, event registration, or joining a community call, with no active participation yet
3. **Participation** — active participation or contribution, for example commenting on Slack or asking a question in a call
4. **Sustained participation** — participating regularly, now or recently
5. **Networked participation** — connecting with others in the community, or bringing new people in
6. **Leadership** — taking on responsibility, for example organising a seminar or joining a working group

Each transition corresponds to a strategic question worth asking: how do we reach the right people (Discovery → First contact), why would people come back (Participation → Sustained participation), and how do we make engagement sustainable and resilient (Networked participation → Leadership)?

Progression up and down the mountain is a descriptive signal for an RCM to notice and respond to, not a target to be maximised — see `why-crm.md` for why this distinction matters.

## A Framework of Open Practices

Developed by [Sharan et al. (2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00108), this offers a complementary lens focused on the community's own practice, rather than an individual's trajectory:


1. Inform community
1. Invite feedback
1. Engage and involve
1. Mobilise and connect
1. Empower groups
1. Decentralise power


Alongside this progression, a set of behaviours — gifting, creating together, soliciting ideas, learning through use, enhancing value exchange, and networking common interests — is attributed to Mozilla Open Innovation and the Copenhagen Institute for Interaction Design.

Where the Mountain of Engagement describes how an individual moves toward leadership, this framework describes how a community's own practices can evolve to make that progression possible — moving from simply broadcasting information outward, toward genuinely sharing power with the community.

## Influence and interest

Adapted from the combined RCM/RAM stakeholder mapping templates, used to prioritise engagement effort:

**Level of influence over the programme**
- *Low* — has little impact on decision-making or the programme's success
- *Medium* — has some impact
- *High* — has high impact on decision-making or success

**Interest in the programme**
- *Low* — there is awareness, but no specific engagement attempts, or attempts have been unsuccessful
- *Medium* — they have engaged, but are not proactively seeking further opportunities
- *High* — they have repeatedly engaged and directed opportunities back to us

**How to engage, based on the combination of influence and interest**
- **Keep informed** — disseminate updates at an appropriate level
- **Keep satisfied** — high influence, lower interest
- **Manage closely** — high influence, high interest
- **Monitor** — follow activity to anticipate needs

## How these frameworks combine in the CRM

A contact's record combines a Mountain of Engagement stage (with full history, not just current value), an influence/interest classification, and a recommended engagement approach.
None of these are fixed once assigned — all are expected to change as the relationship develops, and the CRM's stage-history audit trail exists specifically to make that change visible over time, rather than only showing a single current snapshot.

## A note on measuring engagement fairly

Raw activity counts, used naively, risk systematically under-representing people with less time, fewer resources, or less fluency in the community's dominant working language.
Any rubric built on these frameworks needs deliberate weighting and documentation, not a black-box score — see the ethical considerations in `architecture-technical.md` and the original discussion in the unsuccessful Astera Open Science Fellowship application.
