# RCM-CRM — What It Is, In Plain Language

## What is this?

The RCM-CRM is a tool built by the RCM Cooperative to help research community managers keep track of the people, organisations, and relationships involved in the communities they support.

It is not a sales tool, and it is not designed to track or surveil individuals.
It exists to replace scattered spreadsheets and one person's memory with a shared, structured record — so that community knowledge survives staff changes, and so that community managers can spend more time on relationships and less time on administrative tracking.

## What information does it hold?

Basic contact and organisational details, a record of interactions (for example, attending an event, contributing to a discussion, or publishing something related to the community), and a general sense of how involved someone currently is with the community.

## Why do we track "how involved" someone is?

Understanding "how involved" someone is helps community managers notice where support might be useful: someone new who might benefit from an introduction, someone who used to be active but has gone quiet, or someone keen to take on more responsibility if given the opportunity.

## What don't we do?

- We don't monitor people's activity outside channels they've chosen to participate in.
- We don't share identifiable data publicly without explicit consent.
- We don't use this system to make automated decisions about people; any suggestion the system makes is reviewed by a human before acting on it.
- We don't sell, license, or otherwise commercially exploit the data people share with us.

## Who can see this data?

Access is limited to the team member(s) managing the specific community the tool is used for, unless a person has explicitly consented to broader or public visibility (for example, appearing in a public community map).

## Why build our own tool rather than use an existing one?

Off-the-shelf Customer Relationship Management systems (CRMs) are built for sales or donor relationships.
Research communities are different: identity is often tied to things like ORCID, contributions look like GitHub commits or conference talks rather than purchases, and the ethical bar for how we handle people's data is higher than a typical commercial context requires.
Building our own tool let us design around those specifics, and around our own commitment to transparency, consent, and data minimisation, from the start.
